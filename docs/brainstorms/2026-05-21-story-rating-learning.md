# Story Rating Learning Notes

## Current Taste Signals

- Practical parent actions matter more than poetic emotional validation.
- A good behavior story shows a concrete parent intervention that a real parent recognizes as likely to work.
- Valid parent interventions may include choices, countdowns, first/then framing, rewards, clear boundaries, helper jobs, and simple incentives.
- Rewards are acceptable when they match how real parents solve the moment; do not automatically penalize dessert, stickers, or other incentives.
- The child should usually make a visible next-step behavior change by the end of the story.
- Avoid cute or symbolic fixes when they replace a real action plan.
- Be nitpicky about physical safety and literal wording. If a line can be unsafe, odd, or nonsensical, mark the story down.
- For food stories, smelling food can be rejected if it feels unsafe or pointless. The story should move toward eating, tasting, or a clear family rule.
- Avoid awkward adult phrases such as "thanks for trying with your body."
- For cleanup or toy behavior, the parent instruction must match the real desired action. If the goal is cleanup, say the toy goes in the basket; acceptable choices can be "drop it in" or "place it in gently," not unrelated alternatives.
- Choices must be logical and directly useful. A choice is bad if both options avoid the actual goal or confuse the rule.
- Empathy-based stories can be good when the emotional consequence is simple and parent-recognizable, such as a child noticing a thrown teddy and repairing with a hug. Do not reject these just because they are gentle.
- Simple moral language can still be acceptable for ages 2-5 when the story has a clear wrong action, repair action, and happy social result. Do not over-penalize lines like "sharing makes everyone happy" if the story remains practical and child-readable.
- Playful pretend explanations can be good when they are age-appropriate, stop the behavior, and give the child a concrete replacement action. Do not reject a story only because the strategy is imaginative, such as "the tablet needs sleep," if it works as a parent-recognizable deflection.

## Early Examples

- Bad: A transition story where the shovel "sleeps by the tree" but the parent strategy is too abstract and not operational enough.
- Good: A transition story using a clear choice like "stomp or hop to the gate."
- Good: A transition or eating story using a reward or consequence when it creates clear cause and effect.
- Fixable: A dinner-helper story can work, but the closing line must sound like something a parent would actually say.
- Bad: A thrown-block story where the parent says blocks can stay "in the basket or on the floor" when the desired behavior is putting the block in the basket. The choice should be between two acceptable ways to complete the same task.
- Good: A thrown-toy story where a toy is removed briefly, the child gets an acceptable physical outlet like kicking a pillow, and then tries the toy again safely.
- Good: A toy-grabbing story can use direct social language such as "sharing makes everyone happy" when the child returns the toy and the peer relationship repairs clearly.
- Good: A screen-refusal story can use pretend play like "the tablet is sleeping" if the child accepts it, stops screaming, and moves into a clear calming action.

## 2026-05-22 Sequential Loop Learnings

- Good choices must be two acceptable paths to the desired behavior. For cleanup, "drop it in" or "place it gently" is good; "basket or floor" is bad.
- The parent should never reward the problem behavior. A reward is good only after the child does the requested next step, not after screaming, running, grabbing, or refusing.
- Safety moments need an immediate boundary before any choice. For streets, parking lots, or unsafe hands, the parent should stop, hold, carry, or block first, then offer a small safe choice.
- Playful, empathy-based, or moral framing is acceptable only when it causes a concrete repair action. The story still needs the child to put the toy away, return the toy, take a bite, walk to the gate, or use safer hands.
- Avoid parent actions that train the wrong behavior, especially chasing a child who runs away, bargaining during safety risk, or turning refusal into a game that delays the target action.
- A strong story has this chain: problem behavior -> clear parent action -> child practices the desired behavior -> happy or calm result.
- The parent action should not be a generic "calm parent" move. It should match a mainstream expert strategy for the exact behavior: CDC/AAP-style clear direction and positive discipline, Montessori-style freedom inside limits, positive behavior replacement, or a child-behavior expert repair strategy.
- For rude tone, the story must stay about communication repair. The parent should calmly hold the boundary, model the replacement request, and let the child retry with kind or respectful words. A story that turns rude tone into cup/sip/object compliance is bad.

## Prompt Rule Candidates

- Choose one practical parent strategy before writing: logical choice, first/then, countdown, clear boundary, helper job, reward after behavior, repair action, or playful deflection with a concrete next step.
- Choose the behavior-specific expert strategy before writing, and reject the story if the parent action does not match that strategy.
- Reject any draft where the parent strategy would make the real-world behavior worse.
- Reject any draft where the child never performs the desired next step.
- Reject unsafe sensory actions such as smelling pee or food in a way that could be gross, unsafe, or pointless.
- Keep parent dialogue literal and usable. Avoid vague praise like "thanks for trying with your body."
